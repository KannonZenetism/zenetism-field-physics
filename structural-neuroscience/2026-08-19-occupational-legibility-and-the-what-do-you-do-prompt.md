# Occupational Legibility and the "What Do You Do?" Prompt

The ordinary social question:

> "What do you do?"

is commonly treated as a neutral invitation to talk about oneself.

But the question already contains a frame.

It assumes that occupation is an appropriate proxy for self-description and makes economic or productive function one of the first categories through which a person becomes socially legible.

The claim here is functional, not motive-based.

A person asking the question need not consciously think:

> "I am going to determine this person's social standing."

Social conditioning can make the frame automatic. Occupation carries information associated with income, education, professional standing, productivity, social recognition, and economic position. Once occupation is made the opening category, those relations enter the interaction whether or not the person asking consciously intends to measure them.

Intent and function are therefore distinct.

The frame does not cease to function because the person imposing it is unaware that they are doing so. Social conditioning can reproduce status-indexing without a conscious intention to size another person up.

Even where no deliberate status-assessment occurs, the person being asked is still required to enter an occupational frame they may never have chosen as a meaningful description of themselves.

For some neurodivergent people, this can produce a particularly strong mismatch.

The ordinary assumption is often that asking about someone's occupation is giving them an opportunity to "talk about themselves."

But that contains another assumption:

> **talking about one's occupation is talking about oneself**

That identification does not hold for everyone.

A person may work at something without understanding that work as an identity. A person may have no occupation that adequately expresses who they are. A person may regard intellectual, relational, creative, contemplative, or other dimensions of life as far more self-descriptive than economic function.

The discomfort is therefore not necessarily reluctance to talk about oneself.

It may be reluctance to accept **occupation as the imposed category through which the self must first become legible**.

Rejecting that frame is not the same thing as placing the people who participate in it into another box.

There is a structural difference between:

> **"You belong in this box."**

and:

> **"I reject the box as the required frame through which I must become legible to you."**

The latter is a refusal of compulsory categorization.

A person may have no intention of forcing another into a status-coded frame. But once the conventional prompt makes occupation the expected disclosure, the frame has already been supplied. The person answering must either comply with it, redirect it, refuse it, or bear the social friction produced by not answering in the expected way.

The consequence therefore does not depend upon malicious intent.

The frame can be coercive in function while ordinary in intention.

A less presumptive social relation would begin with comfort rather than compulsory self-disclosure. Interests, work, ideas, experiences, silence, or other aspects of the person could become relevant as the interaction develops.

If someone wants to discuss an occupation, they can.

If occupation genuinely matters to someone's self-understanding, it can emerge naturally.

It does not need to stand in for the person before the person has chosen to make it relevant.

> **Occupational legibility is not personal legibility.**

> **Implicit status-indexing does not require conscious status judgment.**

> **Refusing a social box is not the same thing as placing others inside one.**

> **Social conditioning can reproduce a frame without conscious intent to impose it.**

> **An invitation becomes compulsory framing when one category of self-description is treated as the expected route to social legibility.**
