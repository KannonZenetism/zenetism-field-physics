# The Relatively Real, the Absolutely Real, and the Limits of Knowing

The distinction between what is uncertain, what is unknown, and what is supposedly known to be unknowable intersects with several related but distinct principles I have developed within Zenetism.

**Gnostic-Agnosticism** preserves direct experiential participation while suspending final interpretive certainty. One may experience, participate, or encounter something without claiming exhaustive knowledge of what that experience ultimately is.

**Radical Skepticism** recognizes that perception, interpretation, and every framework of understanding remain conditionally mediated. Scientific, religious, philosophical, and metaphysical frameworks are all subject to this examination, including Zenetism itself.

**Radical Agnosis**, developed through my Agnostic Tetralemma, extends the inquiry further. It questions the epistemic standing of the knower, knowing, the known, affirmation, negation, certainty, uncertainty, and even the act of suspending judgment. It applies that examination to itself.

Consequently, I cannot declare with final certainty that something is absolutely unknowable. Such a declaration would itself require certainty concerning the limits of knowing.

An important distinction in my metaphysical architecture is that between **Relative Structure and Absolute Structure**. Relative Structure concerns particular configurations and expressions, while Absolute Structure, which I name Structon, concerns the invariant condition of structural possibility.

A corresponding distinction arises in epistemology between **the relatively real and the absolutely Real**.

For instance, we can say that "the Sun will rise tomorrow" and arrive at a highly reliable prediction within the framework of our present understanding. That concerns relative reality: the apparent Sun, its apparent motion, our observations, and the temporal and spatial relations through which we describe the event.

But what is the Sun ultimately? What is rising? What is time? What is perception? What is the observer? Do the structures we apprehend correspond to reality as it is, or are they appearances conditioned by faculties whose ultimate reliability remains unestablished?

Those are questions concerning the absolutely Real rather than simply the relative predictability of events.

Consider a destroyed tree. We may observe a tree, examine its remains, and establish reliable findings concerning its destruction within an empirical framework. But that does not settle whether the tree possesses the absolute reality we attribute to it, whether what we call perception corresponds to an actual relation with reality, or whether even the apparent observer has the ontological standing assumed.

We may know that we *appear* to perceive. But what is appearing? What is perceiving? Could something apprehended with extraordinary clarity nevertheless be mistaken for what it is not?

Even if I personally encountered something absolutely Real, how would I establish that I had done so? And how would I demonstrate its absolute validity to another apparent subject whose perceptual and experiential conditions may differ from mine? Indeed, the nature and standing of that other subject remain subject to the same inquiry.

This raises a more fundamental question: **What is realness? Is there an essence of the Real?** My metaphysical architecture articulates essence extensively, but its formulations remain subject to the same epistemic humility. Zenetism is a bridge, not a declaration that reality has been finally captured.

This is why I distinguish limitations of knowledge within a particular framework from absolutely established impossibilities of knowing. The destruction of a tree may demonstrate that a particular historical configuration cannot be reconstructed through available evidence. But declaring it unknowable in every conceivable sense is a further proposition whose ultimate standing remains open.

The philosophical distinction between accepted theories and the Real invites a related inquiry. My examination extends to the grounds upon which we recognize something as Real, determine that its nature is inaccessible, or declare that inaccessibility final.

Within Zenetism, 🕳️ Zenon names the Trans-structural Unknown Principle. But even "Unknown" is an observer-side designation of the limit of discernment, not a final characterization of Zenon-in-itself.

These epistemic distinctions also inform my treatment of emergence. Within Zenetism, potentiality, emergence, emanation, and actualization possess distinct functions within a larger metaphysical architecture. Comparisons with complexity theory may illuminate points of contact and difference while preserving the distinct functions and premises of each account.

The inquiry remains open, including inquiry into the framework through which I articulate it.
